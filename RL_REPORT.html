Leaper RL Study

Hypothesis, setup, and run-by-run findings for teaching the Leaper hexapod to cross an obstacle arena with PPO reinforcement learning. Covers PPO_1 through PPO_12.

Hypothesis — what are we doing?

A PPO policy should be able to teach the hexapod (Leaper) to cross an obstacle-laid arena on its own, just from rewards for getting closer to the target and for reaching it — no scripted spoon-feeding. If it moves far, avoids getting stuck, and completes the mission, we win.

1. Setup summary — How did we do it?

Two connected environments

  • JavaScript / Three.js: displayed the hexapod, arena, obstacles, target, movement, and saved training replays in the browser.
  • Python / Gymnasium: ran the actual RL training, using a faster, simplified top-down representation of the same task.

The Python environment did not simulate detailed joints, balance, motors, or realistic leg physics. It trained the robot as a moving body with collision points representing its body and legs.

Training conditions

  • Arena: 50 × 50 units (coordinates run from −25 to +25 on each axis)
  • Target: fixed at (18, 18)
  • Obstacles: five fixed circular obstacles
  • Starting point: random valid position and direction each attempt
  • Attempt length: maximum 400 steps
  • Maximum movement: 0.75 units per step
  • Maximum rotation: 18° per step
  • Success: reaching the target area
  • Collision (through PPO_11): movement and rotation both rejected, but the attempt continued (changed in PPO_12)

The final collision model checked one body point plus three points on each of six legs — 19 collision points in total.

What the model could control and observe

The robot controlled forward / reverse movement and left / right turning. By PPO_9 it received ten pieces of information: its position; direction and distance to the target; its facing direction; whether it had just collided; and its previous movement and turning commands.

At this stage it did not have cameras, obstacle-distance sensors, a map, or leg-joint information. (Forward-looking obstacle sensors are added later, in PPO_11.)

Reward system

  • Small reward for moving closer to the target; penalty for moving farther away
  • −0.01 per step
  • −0.18 per collision
  • +25 for reaching the target

Training stack

  • Algorithm: PPO; eight environments in parallel
  • 8,192 experiences collected before each learning update; ten learning passes per update
  • Seed 7 (fixed through PPO_11; from PPO_12 each run uses a fresh natural random seed)
  • Checkpoint evaluation every 10,000 steps
  • Python, Gymnasium, Stable-Baselines3, PyTorch — on CPU
  • TensorBoard + Matplotlib for learning curves; Three.js for browser replay

Outcomes — what did we find?

PPO_1–2: proving the basic task was learnable

PPO_1 (24,576 steps) confirmed the pipeline, logging, and model saving worked. PPO_2 (303,104 steps) reached about 60% success at its best archived checkpoint and 57% in the final 100-attempt evaluation. Strong — but only the central body could collide; its legs passed through obstacles. It proved PPO could navigate the simplified arena, not that it could control the realistic collision-aware hexapod.

PPO_3–4: realistic leg collisions changed the problem

PPO_3 added 18 leg checks (19 points total) and reached only 4% success before being stopped at 229,376 steps. PPO_4 trained the full model for 303,104 steps and reached 7% success with mean reward −46.23 (4% at the 300k checkpoint).

The first dramatic result: adding legs dropped performance from 57% to single digits, and 300k steps were not enough to recover. When any point collided, both movement and turning were rejected — so the robot could get stuck repeatedly attempting the same blocked action.

PPO_5–8: reward changes improved scores, but not reliable behavior

  • PPO_5 — exploratory (106,496 steps); reward change and final eval not preserved. Not a formal conclusion.
  • PPO_6 — time-decaying goal reward (303,104 steps): 0% success, mean reward −17.57.
  • PPO_7 — interrupted at 147,456 steps; reward experiment later withdrawn.
  • PPO_8 — the final documented reward system (progress + time + collision + goal), 303,104 steps: still 0% deterministic success.

PPO_8's average training reward improved (≈ −59.53 over the first 100 attempts → ≈ −25.94 over the last 100), yet every deterministic checkpoint reported 0%. The score improved while exploratory randomness was active; the final policy produced no useful movement without it.

PPO_8's major finding: the robot learned to freeze

  • Timed out in 100% of deterministic attempts
  • Zero throttle on ≈ 99% of steps; effectively zero progress
  • Usually stood still rather than risk a collision; occasionally stuck in a collision loop until timeout

Standing still was comparatively safe: moving risked the collision penalty, stopping only cost the smaller time penalty. The target was reachable (a typical start needs ≈ 39–43 full-speed steps vs a 400-step limit), so the problem was the strategy, not time or reachability.

PPO_9: collision memory helped exploration, not deployment

PPO_9 added three observations: whether the last action collided, and the previous movement and turning commands. Late in training, exploratory success reached ≈ 10% and mean progress ≈ 6.65 units. But the final deterministic evaluation was still 0% success, 100% stopped steps, zero progress, mean reward −6.03. It isolated the one-sided forward control as the likely cause of the freeze.

PPO_10: reverse movement finally broke the freeze

One controlled change: movement became a signed control (negative = reverse, 0 = stop, positive = forward).

The latest 100 exploratory attempts reached 34% success and 16.14 units of progress (mean reward ≈ −0.48) — kept separate from the stricter 5% deterministic result. PPO_10 moved and approached the target but still could not reliably escape obstacles, having no way to sense them before contact.

PPO_11: giving the robot sight — the breakthrough

One controlled change: eight obstacle-clearance rangefinder rays, fanning out every 45° around the facing (one straight ahead), each reporting clear distance to the nearest obstacle or wall. The observation grew from 10 to 18 values. We never told it to avoid obstacles — it only gained the readings and learned avoidance from the same −0.18 penalty.

69% deterministic success is the best in the project's history — higher even than the body-only PPO_2 (57–60%) in the much harder 19-point arena. The robot now reaches the target and ends the attempt rather than timing out.

Two honest caveats: the policy settled on a mostly-reverse gait (backing toward the target scores just as well — cosmetic, since net progress is strongly positive), and 31% of attempts still time out, with one stuck in a full 400-step collision streak.

  1. The rays are confirmed, decisively. All three seeds tower over blind PPO_10 (5%); stochastic success is high and tight (83–93%). The jump is the sensor, not luck.
  2. Getting unstuck is still not solved, and it's seed-sensitive. Success spans 39–69% (mean ≈ 54%); seeds 11 and 23 collide ~43% of the time. Seed 7 was simply luckiest on collisions. Every seed still has ~400-step streak attempts.

The mechanism: a blocked step rejected both turning and moving, so a deterministic policy re-issued the same rejected action and looped until timeout, while exploratory randomness escaped.

PPO_12: letting a stuck robot wriggle free

Kept the rays; changed only the collision response. When a combined turn-and-move is blocked, the two now resolve independently — rotate in place if that alone is clear, then move along the resolved facing if that alone is clear. A touching robot can finally turn or reverse out of contact. Reward semantics unchanged. First run to use a fresh natural seed (53054).

A modest, real improvement rather than a knockout. What worked: the escape mechanic is proven in unit tests — a wedged robot rotates or reverses out instead of freezing; success (65%) sits at the strong end of PPO_11's spread and above its ~54% seed average, and typical jams are much shorter (62.8 vs 92.7 / 112.0). What didn't: the collision rate did not fall (40.47%), and one attempt still hit a 400-step streak — partly a measurement artifact, since the flag marks any blocked intended move even when the robot then wriggles out.

The lesson: avoiding contact is a different skill from escaping it.

Overall findings

  1. The simplified task was learnable — PPO_2 did well, but body-only collisions weren't realistic enough.
  2. Leg collisions became the primary difficulty — 57% → single digits once six legs were included.
  3. Reverse movement solved the freeze, but not navigation — PPO_10 moved and approached, but couldn't reliably escape obstacles.
  4. Giving the robot sight was the breakthrough — eight rays took success from 5% to 69%, confirmed across three seeds.
  5. Escaping contact is a separate, still-open skill — decoupling the collision response (PPO_12) shortens jams but didn't lower the collision rate on its own.

The training curves tell the same story: for several runs, exploratory reward improved without matching deterministic success. PPO_10 was the first run where better training behavior also produced active deterministic movement; PPO_11 was where deterministic performance finally jumped.

Where we stand and what's next

The rays and the decoupled collision response are permanent keepers. The remaining problem is contact avoidance, not escape. The next single controlled experiment (PPO_13) attacks it directly: raise the collision penalty above −0.18 so grinding along an obstacle costs more reward, keeping the rays, the decoupled physics, and all PPO settings unchanged. An alternative single change is denser or longer-range rays to see narrow gaps. As always, we test only one thing at a time.
